# Win Indian Kitchens With Private, Honest Nutrition

FITme should not try to beat MyFitnessPal on database size. Its best move is to become the most trustworthy way to log **homemade Indian food on an iPhone without handing over your data**. That means five things: household units (the 200 ml "medium katori" defined by India's National Institute of Nutrition), a one-tap "how much oil/ghee?" question, saved personal recipes ("my dal"), an on-device AI that *names* the foods while a vetted local database *supplies* the numbers, and default targets taken from the ICMR-NIN *Dietary Guidelines for Indians 2024*, with safety guardrails built in. The AI-estimation research suggested grounding numbers in the Indian Nutrient Databank (INDB), but the food-data research found that INDB is built mostly on IFCT 2017, and IFCT forbids electronic reuse "for creating a product" without written NIN permission. The legally safe path is therefore to build your own Indian recipe set from public-domain USDA and openly licensed UK ingredient data, and to ask NIN and the INDB authors for permission in parallel. Three regulatory exposures need fixing before any new nutrition feature ships: the "Ayurvedic remedy" output on the medicine scanner, the "simplified health value interpretations" wording on the lab scanner, and Apple's rule that healthcare apps come from a legal entity rather than an individual developer. On money, users expect basic logging and scanning to be free and pay for depth. In India, regional pricing at roughly ₹1,499/year is about half the US median, so it has to be set by hand. This report is research synthesis, not legal or medical advice. Items marked [unverified] in the underlying notes keep that marker here and should be checked before they reach in-app copy.

## Incumbents paywall the basics while India's leader sells coaching

The global nutrition-tracker market has split into four camps. The large database-plus-ads incumbents keep moving core logging behind paywalls. Premium "algorithm" apps have no free tier. Accuracy-focused apps keep generous free tiers. AI-photo apps compete on low prices. MyFitnessPal (MFP) moved barcode scanning from free to Premium in October 2022, a change that "upset millions of free users" ([Pocket-lint](https://www.pocket-lint.com/apps/news/162386-wow-myfitnesspal-put-its-popular-barcode-scanner-feature-behind-a-paywall/)). It now charges **$79.99/year** for Premium, which unlocks photo, barcode and voice logging and custom macro goals ([FitBudd](https://www.fitbudd.com/post/myfitnesspal-app-cost)). In March 2026 MFP announced it had acquired Cal AI, the viral photo-calorie app, which had **more than $40M in sales over the trailing 12 months** ([MFP blog](https://news.myfitnesspal.com/myfitnesspal-expands-its-position-as-the-leading-player-in-digital-nutrition-tracking-with-cal-ai-acquisition/)). The photo-AI segment is consolidating under a cloud-database incumbent. Lose It! reportedly doubled its annual price to $79.99 and paywalled macros [unverified: single competitor-blog source] ([NutriScan](https://nutriscan.app/blog/posts/cheapest-nutrition-app-2026-pricing-compared-4c18a9205d)). MacroFactor charges **$71.99/year with no free tier** for its adaptive calorie target ([FeastGood](https://feastgood.com/macrofactor-vs-cronometer-2/)). Cronometer Gold costs $49.99/year on top of a free tier reviewers rate as strong ([FeastGood](https://feastgood.com/macrofactor-vs-cronometer-2/)).

Most other Indian players are coaching or hardware businesses. Fitelo made ₹97.3 Cr from diet coaching in FY25 ([Inc42](https://inc42.com/company/fitelo/)), and Sugar.fit grew 77% to ₹66.5 Cr on glucose monitors plus coaching ([Entrackr](https://entrackr.com/fintrackr/sugarfit-posts-77-revenue-growth-in-fy25-narrows-losses-11002610)). Being "India-aware" is no longer a moat on its own. The India diet-app market is small in dollars but growing: about **$109M in 2024, projected to reach $257M by 2030** [analyst estimate from a search snippet] ([Grand View Research](https://www.grandviewresearch.com/horizon/outlook/diet-and-nutrition-apps-market/india)).

FITme's defensible position is the *combination* no one else offers. It runs on-device with no account, handles Indian food, combines workouts with meals, syncs with Apple Health, and has no sales calls. Privacy is a documented pain point, not a hypothetical one. Fitness and calorie apps collect an average of **15.2 data points, 72.9% of them non-optional** ([Incogni](https://blog.incogni.com/fitness-apps-privacy-research/)), and Lose It!, Noom and WeightWatchers sit in Surfshark's high-tracking group ([Surfshark](https://surfshark.com/research/chart/weight-control-apps-privacy)). In an Indian smartphone-buyer survey, **61% said on-device AI improves privacy** ([Free Press Journal](https://www.freepressjournal.in/tech/68-consumers-prioritise-ai-features-while-buying-smartphones-82-value-data-privacy-cmr-report)). Only **3% of smartphone owners would pay extra for AI features** ([eMarketer](https://www.emarketer.com/content/interest-on-device-ai-continues-decline-consumers-arent-willing-pay)), so privacy and AI should be how FITme earns trust, not things it charges for. One honest constraint follows: with a 60+ item food database, FITme cannot compete on breadth. It should describe itself as "AI estimates plus your own saved foods", not as a big database.

## Indian homemade food breaks trackers through oil, portions and recipes

The complaints that recur across apps are paywall creep on basics, intrusive free-tier ads, crowdsourced database errors and duplicates, and AI photo estimates users don't trust ([Nutrola Reddit summary, secondary](https://nutrola.app/en/blog/what-do-reddit-users-say-about-lose-it-2026)). For Indian users there is a further layer. Generic apps log dal as "lentil soup", may not have roti at all, and ignore the ghee tadka. Users want to log in katoris and roti counts ([VoCal](https://www.vo-cal.com/blog/best-calorie-tracking-apps-indian-food-2026); [CalFix](https://calfix.app/blog/best-calorie-tracker-indian-food)). The real failure for Indian home food is *recipe and portion variance*, not recognizing the dish. Everyone's dal is a different thickness with a different amount of ghee. A saved "my dal" that remembers the user's own katori and oil level is worth more than another thousand database rows. That point comes from reasoning across the notes, not from a measured study.

The accuracy literature points the same way. The National Institutes of Health (NIH) tested MyFitnessPal, Lose It!, Cal AI and Appediet on 102 meals weighed to 0.1 g in a metabolic kitchen. **All four underestimated energy by about 250–345 kcal per meal and fat by about 30 g per meal** ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/07/260726015237.htm); [Medscape](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/photo-based-meal-apps-underestimate-calories-and-fat-2026a1000qo9)). This is a conference abstract, not yet a peer-reviewed paper. Hidden cooking fat is the main culprit, and in Indian food the fat is dissolved into the dal or curry where no camera can see it. A cut of 30 g of fat a meal is roughly 270 kcal. A user who thinks they are in a 500 kcal deficit can easily be eating at maintenance and conclude that "calorie counting doesn't work for me". That is why one question matters more than any other feature: **"Oil/ghee: none / light / normal / restaurant"**. One teaspoon of oil (5 g, per NIN's measure) carries about 45 kcal (9 kcal/g × 5 g, simple arithmetic), and 1–3 teaspoons per serving is a plausible home range.

What users find acceptable is also documented. A 2026 scoping review of calorie-counting apps for adults with weight-related chronic disease found the most-cited acceptability factors were **personalization, automation, user-friendly design and data sharing with healthcare professionals** ([JMIR mHealth 2026](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2026/1/e64139)). The research did not find published cross-app data quantifying how much retention comes from recent foods, copy-yesterday, quick-add or saved meals. They are standard user expectations, cheap to build, and the notes treat them as table stakes.

## ICMR-NIN 2024 gives FITme a ready-made, defensible rulebook

FITme does not need to invent its nutrition rules. India's own *Dietary Guidelines for Indians 2024* (DGI 2024) supply most of them in quotable form, and a 2025 India Obesity Commission paper adds Asian body-size cut-offs. Building on Indian national guidance gives FITme a defensible answer to "why is my target X?" and sets it apart from apps that apply US norms. A few terms first. **RMR/BMR** is the calories your body burns at rest. **TDEE** (total daily energy expenditure) is RMR plus everything you burn through activity, i.e. your maintenance calories. A **deficit** is eating below TDEE.

### Starting calories: Mifflin-St Jeor, then let the data take over

Mifflin-St Jeor is the most reliable general resting-calorie equation. It came within 10% of measured values in about 82% of non-obese and 75% of obese people [unverified exact percentages] ([Frankenfield 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15883556/)), and a later study confirmed it as most accurate ([Clinical Nutrition 2013](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0261561413001003)). It was validated mostly on white subjects and "may not be accurate" for other groups ([ANDEAL](https://www.andeal.org/template.cfm?template=guide_summary&key=621)). The research found no Indian validation study, so treat day-one targets as a starting guess. After two to three weeks, switch to an **adaptive TDEE** that learns from the user's own logs and weight trend, the approach MacroFactor charges for. MacroFactor describes its estimate as "a deterministic calculation based on your calorie intake and change in trend weight" and warns that partial-day logging biases it ([MacroFactor help](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/20-expenditure)). The classic 7,700 kcal-per-kg rule overstates long-term weight loss because expenditure changes as weight falls ([Hall 2008](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/5991461_What_is_the_Required_Energy_Deficit_per_unit_Weight_Loss)). Small weekly corrections from real data are what handle that.

The formulas to encode are below. Activity multipliers are a widely used convention, not a single validated source [unverified origin].

```
RMR (men)   = 10 × weight_kg + 6.25 × height_cm − 5 × age + 5
RMR (women) = 10 × weight_kg + 6.25 × height_cm − 5 × age − 161
Day-0 TDEE  = RMR × activity factor
              (sedentary 1.2 · light 1.375 · moderate 1.55 · very 1.725 · extra 1.9)

Weight trend (smooths daily water swings; "Hacker's Diet" method, 10% smoothing):
  trend_today = trend_yesterday + 0.1 × (scale_weight_today − trend_yesterday)

Adaptive TDEE (only after ≥14 days with ≥80% of days marked "fully logged"):
  TDEE_observed = average_daily_intake − (trend_end − trend_start) × 7700 ÷ days
  TDEE_new      = TDEE_old + k × (TDEE_observed − TDEE_old),  k between 0.1 and 0.25 per week
  Cap the weekly change at ±100–150 kcal. Exclude days the user marks "incomplete".
```

The smoothing constant comes from [*The Hacker's Diet*](https://www.fourmilab.ch/hackdiet/) [unverified exact page]. The value of k, the weekly cap and the "fully logged" threshold are design choices from the notes, not published constants. MacroFactor keeps its own constants private, so document FITme's in the app and keep them easy to change. FITme can read weight from Apple Health, so this works without the user weighing in inside the app.

### Rate of loss, deficit ceiling and calorie floor

DGI 2024 is explicit: "Weight reduction diets should not be less than 1000 Kcal/day… A reduction of half a kilogram body weight per week is considered to be safe." It also warns that protein's muscle-protecting effect "is compromised if the energy deficit is more than 40% of daily energy needs" ([DGI 2024](https://nin.res.in/dietaryguidelines/pdfjs/locale/DGI_2024.pdf)). The India Obesity Commission accepts **0.5–1 kg/week** as safe, from a 500–1,000 kcal/day deficit ([Misra et al. 2025](https://www.cmcendovellore.org/pub/2025/revised-definition-of-obesity-in-asian-indians-living-in-india.pdf)). In elite athletes, losing about 0.7% of body weight per week preserved lean mass and strength better than about 1.4% per week [unverified details] ([Garthe 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21558571/)).

### Protein: the Indian diet's biggest gap

ICMR-NIN sets the protein RDA at **0.83 g/kg/day, about 54 g for a 65 kg adult**. It notes that intakes "greater than ~1.6g/kg/day do not contribute any further" to training-induced muscle gain, and that protein powders are "not advisable" as the main route ([DGI 2024](https://nin.res.in/dietaryguidelines/pdfjs/locale/DGI_2024.pdf)). A meta-analysis of 49 trials found muscle gains plateau at **1.62 g/kg/day, with 2.2 g/kg the upper confidence bound** ([Morton et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/)). This matters because Indians eat cereal-heavy, protein-light diets. Cereals supply **50–70% of daily energy**, while pulses, meat, poultry and fish together supply **6–9% against a recommended 14%** ([DGI 2024](https://nin.res.in/dietaryguidelines/pdfjs/locale/DGI_2024.pdf)). A 70 kg vegetarian lifter needs about 112 g/day at 1.6 g/kg, which is hard on a typical Indian plate. NIN's own 2,000 kcal vegetarian "My Plate" reaches only about 72 g ([DGI 2024](https://nin.res.in/dietaryguidelines/pdfjs/locale/DGI_2024.pdf)). A "protein gap" nudge suggesting paneer, curd, dal, chana, soy, eggs or sattu is the most useful India-specific coaching FITme can offer. Take those foods' protein values from FITme's own database, not from memory. NIN also notes that combining cereals and pulses at about **3:1** improves protein quality ([DGI 2024](https://nin.res.in/dietaryguidelines/pdfjs/locale/DGI_2024.pdf)).

A note on water. EFSA's 2.5 L and 2.0 L figures are *total* water, and 70–80% of it normally comes from drinks ([EFSA 2010](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/efsajournal/pub/1459)). Using them as the *drinks* goal is therefore slightly generous. That is reasonable in Indian heat, and it matches ICMR's "two litres/day". Keep a "my doctor limits my fluids" option for heart and kidney patients.

### India-specific insights worth building

Anaemia affects **57% of Indian women and 25% of men aged 15–49** ([Factly, NFHS-5](https://factly.in/data-nfhs-5-findings-reveal-increase-in-anaemia-prevalence-in-children-women-across-most-states/)). Among adolescents, about **31% are B12-deficient and 36% folate-deficient**, with plant- and cereal-based diets linked to deficiency ([Shalini et al. 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10346745/)). FITme can offer a "B12 sources" counter for vegetarians (curd, milk, eggs, fortified foods), with the line "consider asking your doctor about a B12 test". Women can get an iron-focused tip such as "pair iron foods with vitamin C". Do not show supplement doses. Glycaemic index (GI, how fast a food raises blood sugar) changes a great deal with variety, cooking and accompaniments, so do not show per-food GI numbers from the AI. Use meal-level nudges that have Indian trial evidence instead. In overweight Asian Indians, brown rice cut the 24-hour glucose response by **19.8%**, and brown rice plus legumes by **22.9%**, versus white rice ([Mohan et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259845530_Effect_of_Brown_Rice_White_Rice_and_Brown_Rice_with_Legumes_on_Blood_Glucose_and_Insulin_Responses_in_Overweight_Asian_Indians_A_Randomized_Controlled_Trial)). That supports nudges like "add dal to your rice" and "swap part of the rice for brown rice or millets". Intermittent fasting works about as well as ordinary calorie restriction. In a 2025 BMJ network meta-analysis of 99 trials, only alternate-day fasting beat continuous restriction, by **1.29 kg** ([Semnani-Azad et al., BMJ 2025](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/392802015_Intermittent_fasting_strategies_and_their_effects_on_body_weight_and_other_cardiometabolic_risk_factors_systematic_review_and_network_meta-analysis_of_randomised_clinical_trials)). Offer a fasting window (Navratri, Ekadashi, Ramadan) as a scheduling tool within the same calorie target, not as a superior method.

### Guardrails are part of the product, not a legal afterthought

Calorie tracking is linked to eating-disorder harm in vulnerable users. In one study of 105 people with eating disorders, about three-quarters used MyFitnessPal, and about **73% of those users said it contributed to their eating disorder** ([Levinson et al. 2017](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1471015317301484)). The findings are correlational. The American Academy of Pediatrics advises against calorie counting for adolescents ([AAP 2016](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/138/3/e20161649), [unverified URL]). ICMR adds **350 kcal/day in pregnancy**, and advises overweight pregnant women to cut added sugar and refined cereals rather than diet ([DGI 2024](https://nin.res.in/dietaryguidelines/pdfjs/locale/DGI_2024.pdf)). Kidney guidelines restrict protein to roughly 0.55–0.8 g/kg depending on diabetes status [unverified exact numbers] ([KDOQI 2020](https://www.ajkd.org/article/S0272-6386(20)30726-5/fulltext)), so FITme's high-protein defaults would be harmful for CKD patients. Add one onboarding screen and a few background checks:

| Situation | What FITme does |
|---|---|
| Under 18 | No deficit goals and no calorie numbers by default; show food-group / My Plate guidance |
| Pregnant or breastfeeding | Disable deficits; add +350 kcal (pregnancy) or a breastfeeding add-on; prompt to see their doctor |
| Current or past eating disorder | "No-numbers mode", no deficit goals, no streak pressure, no red "over budget" colours, helpline shown (India Tele-MANAS 14416 [unverified, check before shipping]) |
| Diabetes on insulin or sulfonylureas | Warning before any deficit or fasting window: "Cutting calories or fasting can cause low blood sugar with your medicine. Talk to your doctor first." |
| Kidney disease, liver disease or gout | Cap protein at 0.8 g/kg and show a doctor prompt |
| Background checks | Soft check-in (not a block) if BMI < 18.5, goal below BMI 18.5, logged intake < 1,000 kcal for 3+ days, or weekly loss > 1% |

Wording should stay neutral: no "good/bad foods" and no "cheat meals". These guardrails come from expert reasoning. The research found no trial showing which specific app guardrails reduce harm.

## Build your own Indian recipe database from openly licensed ingredients

This is where the two research threads disagreed, and the conflict has to be resolved before writing code. The AI-estimation research recommended giving the on-device model a lookup tool backed by INDB: an open-access databank of **1,095 raw foods and 1,014 Indian recipes**, built mainly on IFCT 2017 ([PMC11277795](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11277795)). Technically that is the right architecture. Legally, that database cannot be the data source today.

IFCT 2017, the National Institute of Nutrition's lab-analysed table of 528 Indian foods, carries this copyright notice: "no part of this publication can be stored or reproduced in any electronic format for creating a product without the prior written permission of the National Institute of Nutrition, Hyderabad" ([NIN IFCT2017.pdf](https://www.nin.res.in/ebooks/IFCT2017.pdf)). INDB's raw-food values come mostly from IFCT 2017, with gaps filled from IFCT 2004, UK and USDA data ([PMC11277795](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11277795)). Anuvaad's download page calls INDB "open-access" but states no licence, no terms and nothing about commercial use or NIN permission ([Anuvaad](https://www.anuvaad.org.in/indian-nutrient-databank/)). The INDB GitHub repo itself says IFCT data "must be requested from the original source" ([GitHub](https://github.com/lindsayjaacks/Indian-Nutrient-Databank-INDB-)). That suggests the authors did not consider IFCT freely redistributable. The paper's CC BY licence covers the *article*, not clearly the IFCT-derived numbers. Kaggle copies that relabel INDB as CC BY-SA 4.0 ([Kaggle](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/batthulavinay/indian-food-nutrition)) add risk and remove none, because an uploader cannot license rights they don't hold. So **do not bundle INDB, IFCT or any Kaggle "Indian food nutrition" file in a paid app until you hold written permission.** Separately, check where the values in FITme's current 60+ items came from. If any were copied from IFCT, INDB or a Kaggle CSV, replace them in the next release.

The safe path keeps the same architecture and changes the data source. Numbers you calculate yourself from openly licensed ingredient data are your own. USDA FoodData Central is **CC0 (public domain): commercial use and offline bundling need no permission**, only a requested citation ([FDC API Guide](http://fdc.nal.usda.gov/api-guide/)). Its Foundation Foods and SR Legacy sets are small, about 3.7 MB and 6.7 MB zipped as CSV ([FDC downloads](http://fdc.nal.usda.gov/download-datasets/)). Trimmed to the nutrients FITme shows, they fit easily in an app. The UK's CoFID dataset is under the **Open Government Licence v3.0** (OGL, which allows commercial use with attribution and no share-alike), is a 4.42 MB Excel file ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/composition-of-foods-integrated-dataset-cofid)), and includes UK-consumed South Asian dishes [count unverified]. INDB itself used it for 144 ingredients. Canada's nutrient file is also open-licensed ([Open Canada](https://open.canada.ca/data/en/dataset/089885f9-ed53-44e6-854a-14d21a1ec2e0)). Australia's AFCD is share-alike and best skipped ([FSANZ](https://www.foodstandards.gov.au/science-data/monitoringnutrients/afcd/datauserlicenceagreement)).

On top of that ingredient base, write **200–500 standard Indian recipes** yourself, ideally checked by a dietitian. Record each one as raw-ingredient grams (atta, toor dal, onion, oil and so on) plus the cooked total weight, which gives a per-100 g cooked value. Make oil or ghee an editable field, not a hidden constant. This "sum the ingredients, adjust for cooking weight change" approach is the standard FAO/INFOODS and EuroFIR method ([FAO](https://www.fao.org/fileadmin/templates/food_composition/documents/Presentations/Food_Composition_-_Recipes_and_other_calculations.pdf)). FITme can even do better than INDB in one respect. INDB did **not** apply yield factors (the water gained or lost in cooking) ([PMC11277795](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11277795)), so weighing your own cooked dishes fixes that. The trade-off is that USDA and UK data cover Indian-specific ingredients (regional pulses, millets, local vegetables) thinly, so some values will differ from IFCT's lab measurements. Label them "calculated estimate". The research did not measure how many IFCT ingredients have close USDA or UK equivalents.

For portions, use NIN's household measures: **large katori 360 ml, medium 200 ml, small 155 ml and 115 ml, tablespoon 15 g, teaspoon 5 g** ([DGI 2024 Annexure I](https://nin.res.in/dietaryguidelines/pdfjs/locale/DGI_2024.pdf)). Store a grams-per-katori value for each dish, because volume-to-weight differs by food. NIN publishes no standard roti diameter, roti weight or ladle volume, so weigh your own and let users override them ("my rotis are small"). DGI 2024 carries the same restrictive copyright notice as IFCT. Citing six measurement values is very different from bundling a database, but mention the use in your permission request too. This is not legal advice.

For packaged food, the best fit is on-device label reading, not a barcode database. Since the FSSAI Labelling and Display Regulations 2020, Indian packs must print energy, protein, carbohydrate, total and added sugars, total, saturated and trans fat, cholesterol and sodium **per 100 g/ml**, plus %RDA per serving ([FSSAI compendium](https://fssai.gov.in/upload/uploadfiles/files/Comp_Labelling%20Display_Version%20VIII_09_09_2025.pdf)). Apple's Vision text recognition runs on-device ([Apple docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/vision/vnrecognizetextrequest/supportedrecognitionlanguages())). A parser keyed to that fixed field list can read the panel, let the user confirm, and save the result as a personal food. That needs no licence and no network call. Indian labels often use tiny print ([OFF blog](https://blog.openfoodfacts.org/en/news/open-food-facts-india-database-reaches-10k-product-milestone)), so the confirmation step is essential. An optional barcode lookup can call Open Food Facts live. Its database is under ODbL (which requires attribution, and any merged database you distribute must also be released under ODbL), reads are rate-limited to **15 per minute per IP**, and it covered about **10,000 Indian products** as of late 2024 ([OFF data](https://world.openfoodfacts.org/data); [OFF API](https://openfoodfacts.github.io/openfoodfacts-server/api/)). Keep OFF results only in the user's own log, never merged into the bundled database, and disclose that the barcode leaves the phone. Commercial APIs all conflict with "100% on-device". FatSecret's free tier is **US-only** ([FatSecret](https://platform.fatsecret.com/api-editions)). Edamam bans building a local copy ([Edamam](https://developer.edamam.com/food-database-api)). Spoonacular requires deleting caches after one hour ([Spoonacular](https://spoonacular.com/food-api/pricing)). Nutritionix reportedly starts around $499/month [unverified] ([Nutritionix](https://www.nutritionix.com/api)).

In parallel, ask for permission. If NIN grants it, IFCT would sharply improve accuracy for Indian ingredients, and INDB would add 1,014 recipes at once. Send something like this to ifct2017@gmail.com and nin@ap.nic.in (contacts listed in the IFCT PDF), and send a matching request to Anuvaad and Prof. Lindsay Jaacks through the INDB page:

> Subject: Request for written permission to use IFCT 2017 data in a mobile app
>
> Dear Sir/Madam, I am the developer of FITme, an iOS health and fitness app that helps Indian users log home-cooked meals. All processing happens on the user's phone; we collect no personal data. We would like to include nutrient values from the Indian Food Composition Tables 2017 (and the Dietary Guidelines for Indians 2024 household measures) inside the app, with full acknowledgement of NIN-ICMR as the source on an in-app "Data sources" screen. The app has a free version and an optional paid tier. As the IFCT 2017 copyright notice requires prior written permission for electronic products, we request that permission and would be glad to follow any conditions, attribution wording or fee you specify. Thank you, [Name, company, email, app link]

Expect a slow reply or none (an inference). Keep any response on file.

## Let the on-device model name foods and the database supply numbers

The accuracy evidence produces one clear design rule. The AI should identify *what* and *how much*, and arithmetic over vetted data should produce the calories. Large models are good at naming foods. ChatGPT-4 identified foods with **93% precision**, but its portion-weight estimates were off by **27.8% on average**, and it underestimated 76% of meals, especially large plates (530 g estimated versus 798 g actual) ([O'Hara et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11858203/)). Google's Nutrition5k study showed that predicting calories per gram gives 9.5% error, but adding portion estimation nearly triples it to **26.1%** ([arXiv 2103.03375](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.03375)). Context from the user helps more than anything else. In a 195-dish test of ChatGPT-5, average energy error was **30.5% from a photo alone, 24.4% when told the fat and sweetener type, 13.9% with ingredients and quantities, and 18.1% from an ingredient list with no photo** ([Rodríguez-Jiménez et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12655113/)). FITme's current text-with-quantities input is therefore a better starting point than a bare photo. A photo adds value on top of text, not instead of it. A 52-study systematic review found average AI calorie-estimation errors from 0.10% to 38.3%, with individual errors up to 79.6%, and worse results for multi-food images ([Annals of Medicine 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10836267/)). Humans set a low bar: dietitians estimating portions from images were off by about **44–48%** ([PMC6115988](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6115988/)). All these figures come from large cloud models or purpose-trained networks. No study has evaluated Apple's small on-device model on food, so expect it to be worse at *recalling* nutrition numbers.

That shapes the architecture. Apple's Foundation Models framework runs an approximately **3-billion-parameter model on the phone**. Apple says it is "not designed to be a chatbot for general world knowledge" ([Apple ML Research](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/apple-foundation-models-2025-updates)). It is free, offline and adds no app size. It offers *guided generation* (the `@Generable` macro forces output into a Swift struct that always parses) and *tool calling* (the model can call a function you write, such as a food lookup) ([WWDC25](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/286/)). On iOS 26 the on-device context window was about 4K tokens ([secondary explainer](https://blakecrosley.com/blog/apple-foundation-models-framework)). That is far too small to paste a food database into the prompt, so a lookup tool is required anyway. iOS 27 adds **image input** (`Attachment`) plus Vision-backed `OCRTool` and `BarcodeReaderTool` ([WWDC26 session 241](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2026/241/)). The research could not confirm whether image input works offline on every eligible device, so check before building on it. The model only runs on Apple Intelligence hardware (**iPhone 15 Pro and newer**) ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/en-us/121115)), and **Hindi is not a supported language** ([iClarified](https://www.iclarified.com/102432/siri-ai-compatibility-supported-devices-languages-and-availability)). Many Indian users are on older or non-Pro iPhones, so FITme needs a non-AI path that works almost as well.

The pipeline to build has five steps, synthesized across the notes. First, the model turns "2 roti, 1 katori dal, thoda ghee" (or, on iOS 27, a photo) into a structured list: `[FoodItem{name, matchedDbId, quantity, unit, cookingFat, confidence}]`. Second, a `lookupFood` tool matches each item to FITme's own recipe and ingredient database, using a **local synonym table for Hinglish and regional names** (dal/daal/toor/arhar, sabzi/subzi, roti/chapati/phulka). This table also powers the non-AI fallback parser on older phones. Third, the app computes macros deterministically as database value per gram × grams, using the katori, roti and spoon conversion table. Fourth, for low-confidence items or fat-sensitive dishes (dal tadka, curries, paratha, halwa, biryani), it asks at most **two quick-chip questions**: "Oil/ghee: none · light · normal · restaurant" and "How many katoris/rotis?" Fifth, it shows the result labelled "Estimated", lets the user edit grams and items, and remembers corrections such as the user's roti size or home oil level as on-device personal defaults. Because the documented error runs in one direction (too low), default the oil chip to "normal" home cooking rather than zero. That is a deliberate, defensible correction.

Skip LiDAR or depth-sensing volume estimation. It is a large custom-ML project and still cannot see ghee dissolved in dal. Skip shipping a large open model through MLX too: Gemma 3 4B is about 2.1 GB ([arXiv 2601.19139](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.19139)), a heavy storage and battery cost. Don't train a shipped classifier on the Khana Indian food dataset (131K images, up to 86.7% top-1 accuracy). It is **CC BY-NC-ND**, which bars commercial use ([arXiv 2509.06006](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.06006)). It can serve at most as a private evaluation set, subject to its licence. Do not use Apple's Private Cloud Compute as a fallback either. Apple says prompts are not stored, but processing on Apple servers would technically break the "100% on-device" promise that is FITme's main differentiator.

## Ayurveda "remedies" and lab "interpretations" are the real App Store risk

Nutrition logging is ordinary Health & Fitness. FITme's medicine and lab scanners are what could make Apple or a regulator treat it as a healthcare product, and they should be fixed before any diet expansion ships. Three App Store guidelines apply. **Guideline 1.4.1** subjects medical apps that "could provide inaccurate data… or that could be used for diagnosing or treating patients" to greater scrutiny, says accuracy claims that "cannot be validated" will be rejected, and requires apps to "remind users to check with a doctor… before making medical decisions." **Guideline 5.1.1(ix)** says apps providing services in "highly regulated fields (such as… healthcare…)" should be submitted "by a legal entity… and not by an individual developer." **Guideline 5.1.3(ii)** says apps "must not write false or inaccurate data into HealthKit" and "may not store personal health information in iCloud" ([Apple App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). For a solo founder, 5.1.1(ix) is the sharpest exposure. Enrolling in the Apple Developer Program as an organization (a company or LLP with a D-U-N-S number) both reduces that risk and limits personal liability [confirm with a lawyer].

The medicine scanner's "Ayurvedic remedy" output is the single riskiest design in the app. India's Drugs and Magic Remedies Act bans advertising drugs to diagnose, cure or treat scheduled conditions including **diabetes and cancer** ([Karnataka Drugs Control](https://drugs.kar.nic.in/node/136.html)). The pre-approval rule for AYUSH drug ads (Rule 170) **now stands omitted** after the Supreme Court vacated its stay ([Medical Dialogues](https://medicaldialogues.in/news/health/misleading-medical-ads-case-sc-disposes-ima-plea-lifts-stay-on-ayush-ad-pre-approval-rule-153283)). The DMR Act and consumer-protection law still apply, and in 2024–25 ASCI reported **233 misleading health ads** to the AYUSH Ministry, mostly "cure" claims ([The South First](https://thesouthfirst.com/health/from-cancer-cures-to-sexual-weakness-fixes-asci-flags-233-misleading-health-ads-to-ayush-ministry/)). In the US, the FTC's health-claims guidance explicitly covers health apps and "traditional medicine" claims and generally expects randomized-trial evidence ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)). The medical danger is concrete. Ashwagandha may add to metformin's glucose-lowering effect ([Drugs.com](https://www.drugs.com/drug-interactions/ashwaganda-with-metformin-2706-0-1573-0.html)), and sources conflict on whether ashwagandha and guggul raise bleeding risk with warfarin ([Drugs.com](https://www.drugs.com/drug-interactions/ashwaganda-with-warfarin-2706-0-2311-0.html)). A flow that scans a prescribed drug and then suggests an herbal "remedy" implies substitution and invites interaction harm. Whether in-app content counts as an "advertisement" under the DMR Act is unresolved and needs a lawyer. The risk rises sharply if FITme names branded products, uses affiliate links, or ties remedies to scheduled diseases.

The lab-report scanner faces a medical-device line that India, the US and the EU draw in similar places. India's CDSCO 2026 software guidance excludes general-wellness apps but covers software that will "screen, diagnose, alert, monitor or manage a disease" ([LexOrbis](https://www.lexorbis.com/cdsco-issues-guidance-on-medical-device-software-under-the-medical-devices-rules-2017/)). Sources disagree on whether it was issued on 21 July or 13 August 2026. The FDA's January 2026 general-wellness guidance allows apps to "display values, ranges, trends", but notifications may **not** call results abnormal or use clinical thresholds. Only a prompt that "evaluation by a healthcare professional may be helpful" is permitted ([FDA Law Blog](https://www.thefdalawblog.com/2026/01/a-busy-day-in-the-cdrh-neighborhood-updates-to-the-cds-and-general-wellness-guidance-documents/)). Under EU MDR Rule 11, software that informs diagnosis starts at Class IIa ([MDCG 2019-11](https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf)). Running the AI on the device does not change any of this, because device status depends on intended use and claims. The safe version shows each value next to **the reference range printed on the user's own report**, explains terms in plain language ("HbA1c reflects average blood sugar over about three months"), charts trends, and says "Discuss these results with your doctor." The risky version adds FITme's own "High/Abnormal/prediabetic" flags, risk scores, inferred conditions or diet "treatment". **Never connect the lab and Ayurveda features** ("your HbA1c is high, try X"). That would stack the device problem on top of the DMR diabetes problem.

The diet side carries its own claim risk. The FTC acted against Instant Blood Pressure, whose maker claimed cuff-level accuracy after **more than $600,000 in sales** ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2016/12/marketers-blood-pressure-app-settle-ftc-charges-regarding-accuracy-app-readings)), and against two melanoma-detection apps ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2015/02/ftc-cracks-down-marketers-melanoma-detection-apps)). Never publish an accuracy percentage for AI meal estimates without a documented test. Write estimated calories to HealthKit only after the user confirms them.

On privacy, the on-device design is a real advantage, but only if it stays true. India's DPDP Rules phase in core obligations (consent, security, breach reporting, erasure) from **13 May 2027**, and a consent-manager phase starts **13 November 2026** ([ConsentOS](https://consentos.in/learn/dpdp-compliance-timeline/), secondary). The FTC's amended Health Breach Notification Rule covers health apps and treats *unauthorized disclosure*, such as sending data to an ad SDK, as a breach ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/04/ftc-finalizes-changes-health-breach-notification-rule)). If meal photos, OCR text, lab values and logs never leave the phone, FITme's Apple privacy label can arguably read "Data Not Collected" [unverified Apple definition]. Crash or analytics SDKs, RevenueCat purchase identifiers, a cloud AI fallback, an online barcode lookup or a feedback form each change that answer. Audit every SDK before claiming "your data never leaves your iPhone". Offer an encrypted local export to the Files app as the backup route rather than CloudKit sync, given 5.1.3(ii).

## Charge for depth, never for basics, and price India separately

Health & Fitness is among the strongest subscription categories. RevenueCat's 2026 report puts median prices at **$9.99/month and $39.94/year**, with **68% of plans annual** and trial-to-paid conversion around **35–38%** (the report's figures conflict). For India and Southeast Asia, however, the median annual price across all categories is **$18.32 versus $39.99 in North America**, and download-to-paid conversion is **0.7% versus 2.8%** ([RevenueCat SOSA 2026](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps)). Adapty puts India's price index at **0.6** of the US ([Adapty](https://adapty.io/state-of-in-app-subscriptions-report/)). Annual Health & Fitness renewals have a median of just **25%** ([RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/average-subscription-renewal-rates-by-app-category)), so most lifetime value comes from year one. Low-priced annual plans keep **36%** of subscribers after a year, versus 6.7% for high-priced monthly plans, and trials lift Health & Fitness LTV by **63.6%** ([SaaStr summary](https://www.saastr.com/the-top-10-learnings-from-revenuecats-state-of-subscription-apps-how-115000-mobile-apps-deliver-16b-in-revenue-whats-working-whats-quietly-killing-growth/); [arpubrothers summary](https://arpubrothers.com/blog/2025-saas-mobile-apps-trends/)). Adapty finds 90% of trial starts happen on day 0, and one-time (lifetime) purchases rose from 6.4% to **10.3%** of purchases between 2023 and 2025 ([Adapty](https://adapty.io/state-of-in-app-subscriptions-report/)). Lifetime pricing suits FITme especially well: on-device AI has no per-use server cost, so a lifetime buyer doesn't erode margin the way they would in a cloud-AI app.

The free/paid line should follow user anger. MFP's barcode paywall became a long-running grievance, and rival FoodNoms markets a "Free Barcode Scanner" against it ([Droid Life](https://www.droid-life.com/2022/08/24/myfitnesspal-puts-barcode-scanner-behind-premium-paywall/); [FoodNoms](https://foodnoms.com/vs/myfitnesspal)). Keep free: all meal logging including Indian recipes and household units, the oil/ghee chip, saved personal foods and meals, text AI estimates, label scanning, water, default macro targets, workouts, Apple Health sync, encrypted backup, and every safety feature and doctor prompt. Charge for depth: the adaptive TDEE, iOS 27 photo logging, a multi-ingredient recipe builder with cooked-yield and oil maths, trends and India insights (cereal share, protein gap, B12/iron watch), custom per-day macro targets, CSV/PDF reports, widgets and themes. Keeping backup and saved foods free is this report's judgement, not a sourced benchmark. Do not headline the Ayurveda or lab features as Pro selling points. Charging for the riskiest features makes FITme look more like a "healthcare service" to Apple and more like a commercial promoter under the DMR Act and FTC rules.

The price points below are inferences from the benchmarks above, not tested numbers. **US:** $4.99/month, $29.99/year, $59.99 lifetime. That undercuts the $39.94 median because there is no human coach or server, and it sits at Cal AI's reported annual price. **India:** ₹149/month, ₹1,499/year, ₹2,999 lifetime, set manually per storefront (Apple has allowed this since 2023 ([Apple Newsroom](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/pdfs/App-Store-Pricing-Update.pdf))) rather than auto-converted from USD. Confirm the exact allowed INR price points in App Store Connect. Show a dismissible paywall at the end of onboarding with a **7-day trial on the annual plan**, which matches the dominant 5–9-day trial pattern. A hard paywall converts more in RevenueCat's data (10.7% vs 2.1%), but Adapty finds soft paywalls convert better. The sources define these differently, and a hard paywall risks ratings in a market where conversion is already 0.7%. Market FITme in India as "No phone number. No sales calls." That is a direct answer to HealthifyMe's complaint record.

## Conclusion

Taken together, the research shows that FITme's constraints are its strategy. Being on-device rules out a giant cloud food database and a frontier model, and that pushes FITme toward the design the accuracy evidence favours anyway: a small model that names foods, a deterministic database that supplies the numbers, and one or two questions about oil and katoris. Those questions do more for Indian meals than a photo ever will. The INDB conflict follows the same logic. The legally safe route of writing your own recipes from CC0/OGL ingredients is slower, but it produces an asset FITme fully owns, and FITme can measure cooked yields, which INDB never applied. If NIN says yes, that becomes an upgrade rather than a dependency.

The less obvious takeaway is that the riskiest parts of FITme today are not on the diet side at all. They are the Ayurveda and lab features, which push the app toward "healthcare service" in Apple's eyes and toward "advertisement" or "medical device" in regulators' eyes, and which a solo developer enrolled as an individual is least able to defend. Cut those back to education and add the guardrails, and the nutrition expansion can lean fully on what incumbents structurally cannot copy: trust. MFP now owns the leading photo-AI app, and HealthifyMe earns its money from sales-led coaching. Neither is positioned to tell an Indian user "no account, no sales calls, and your dal is logged the way your family makes it." That message, priced well below HealthifyMe's self-serve plans (₹1,499 against the ₹2,499–₹4,999 that unverified secondary sources report), is FITme's opening. None of this is legal or medical advice. Have Indian and US counsel confirm the regulatory points, and check every [unverified] number against its primary source before it appears in the app.
